How does anyone know anything? We collect experiences and moments, little impressions of the world that inform our thoughts and future actions, little nuggets of wisdom that we can share with others. These small artifacts and lessons we accumulate throughout our lives coalesce to form the lens through which we interpret our reality. The words we learn from our mothers become the language we utilize to shape and explore the world around us. Billions of neurons work together to form the machinery that performs an intractable number of computations to give us our sense of reality, our ability to communicate, our ability to think.
How do you know your perception is true? Does what we learn actually reflect the real world? Statistics, a field all about measuring, quantifying, and modeling the world around us, gives us a rigorous framework to test our intuition. It gives structure to our perception of the world, allowing us to distinguish the signal of truth from the noise of coincidence. However, possessing the mathematical framework to understand the world is only half the battle; we also have to gather evidence to give our hypothesis meaning.
How do we discover something new? We collect data, whether through careful experimentation or by observing the world around us. But data is ugly. Data is boring. Data is frustrating. No one enjoys pouring through tables of numbers or staring at a chart full of enough clutter to obscure the very story they are meant to reveal. To make knowledge accessible, we must do more than just collect it; we must curate it. We must transform raw datasets into visual, compelling narratives that allow others to see the patterns we have uncovered.

This portfolio ... They are mirror images of the same pursuit. Just as we use statistical principles to decode the biological complexity of the human brain, we can look to the elegant structures of that very brain to inspire how we build computational models. My ePortfolio maps this cyclical journey: investigating how biological systems process information, and applying those insights to design artificial systems that can learn, see, and understand. By bridging the gap between the organic and the algorithmic, we don't just build better models; we gain a deeper understanding of ourselves.
