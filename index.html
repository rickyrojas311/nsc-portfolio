How does anyone know anything? We collect experiences and moments, little impressions of the world that inform our thoughts and future actions. These small artifacts and lessons we accumulate throughout our lives coalesce to form the lens through which we see our reality. The words we learn as children, the language in which we chose to express ourselves, shape our view of the world around us. Billions of neurons send signals cascading through our brain, enabling the computations that give us our sense of reality, our ability to communicate, and our ability to think.
How do you know your perception is true? hough our ability to learn from the world is powerful, we are often blinded by the sheer randomness of experience. We need a filter—a way to discern whether we’ve found a genuine discovery or if we're simply chasing an illusion. Statistics provides a path to that clarity. By quantifying the chaos around us, it gives structure to our perception and allows us to discover a truth amongst the noise of coincidence.
How do we discover something new? We experiment and observe the world around us, recording data along the way. But data is ugly. Data is frustrating. Data by itself doesn’t reveal or illuminate anything about the world. To make knowledge accessible, we must do more than just collect it; we must curate it. We must transform complex sets of data into visual, compelling narratives that allow others to see the patterns we have uncovered.

This portfolio tells a story of data. Data that is used to learn, teach, and persuade. Through my research as a computational neuroscience, I extract representations of the world from large language models and use them to map the brain’s operations. My work centers on interpretability—designing experiments around a more accessible, dissectable model of cognition to better understand the human brain. But the quest for greater interpretability doesn’t stop at experiment design; it requires taking these results, which are often high dimensional both spatially and temporally, and distilling them into accessible formats, typically data visualizations. As a science communicator, I believe that data visualization is one of the most compelling ways to build stories that are accessible to all, regardless of their background. For technical audiences, effective data visualizations allow for a clear overview of the work and its results. For more general audiences, it allows me to guide them through the project and highlight exciting results without any additional background knowledge.
